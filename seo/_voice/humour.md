# Humour Reference

How this writer uses humor. Derived from the "Writing With Humor" sample and reinforced by the LinkedIn posts. Use this to keep comedic voice consistent across content.

## Core stance
Humor is a **connection tool**, not the main event. The goal is to ingratiate with the reader and earn "street cred," then get back to the point. As the writer puts it: sprinkle it in, don't slather it on.

## The five rules the writer lives by
1. **Be yourself.** Authentic humor beats manufactured jokes; readers can tell the difference. The writer openly likes dad jokes *and* Nickelback and won't apologize for either.
2. **Keep it light-hearted.** Stay away from charged or controversial topics. The more charged the comment, the more likely it alienates a segment of the audience.
3. **Use universal references.** Avoid hyper-specific jokes early; the more general, the safer. Earn the right to niche zingers over time.
4. **Audience-appropriate.** Rate everything PG-13 or lower. Life-experience gaps mean one group's joke sails over another's head — plan for who's actually reading.
5. **Sprinkle it on.** Used sparingly, humor creates memorable moments and helps you stand out. Overused, it loses readers fast.

## Signature comedic devices
- **Dad jokes / groaners, owned proudly** — the "embarrassed 14-year-old son" as a recurring comedic yardstick.
- **Pop-culture opener, remixed** — e.g., riffing on "99 Problems" and flipping the punchline ("my humor ain't one").
- **Extended metaphors** — writing-with-humor compared to *throwing darts* ("happy to find the board" → accuracy with practice); overusing humor compared to *Frank's Red Hot* ("you can't put that stuff on everything").
- **Stage directions in brackets** — `[Switches to serious face]`, `[radio edit]`, `[Switches to serious face]` — used to pivot tone or self-censor for comic effect.
- **Self-deprecation** — "I am by no means a professional comedian," "a glutton for punishment," admitting a joke may have "lost you at the Nickelback comment."
- **Playful self-censoring** — `[radio edit]` / `[radio edit]` in place of profanity, which keeps it PG-13 while nodding at the joke.
- **Callbacks** — the Nickelback bit returns multiple times as a running gag and an audience "filter."
- **Vivid absurd similes** — "a deep belly laugh like a baby that just saw a cat do a backflip."
- **Direct address / breaking the fourth wall** — "The fact that you got to this point clearly shows you are a glutton for punishment."
- **Comic sign-off** — ends on a light, quotable line ("Ain't no party like Joy Youell's tea party").

## LinkedIn-post humor (drier, punchier register)
The posts show a second, tighter comedic mode for social:
- **Deadpan understatement / mock-stakes:** "Buy more credits? You may go into generational debt." · "Figma and Adobe are having a very bad week."
- **Rhetorical bait-and-switch:** poses the obvious fix, then knocks it down ("The solution wasn't paying Anthropic more").
- Short lines, one-idea paragraphs, occasional bold for the punch.

## Do / Don't
**Do:** self-deprecate, use universal pop-culture, own the corny joke, pivot with a bracketed aside, land a memorable closing line, keep it PG-13.
**Don't:** go controversial or political, stack jokes back-to-back, punch down, over-explain a joke, let humor bury the actual information, exceed PG-13.

## Calibration
- **Blog / educational:** ~1 light joke per 200–300 words; always sprinkled, never slathered. Metaphors and callbacks welcome.
- **LinkedIn / social:** drier, punchier, understated; one clean punch per few lines.
- **Serious/sensitive topics:** drop humor entirely (matches "keep it light-hearted, avoid charged topics").
